<h><b>How do I Introduce my foster cat to my pets at home?<b></h>

<p> Introducing a foster cat to your resident pets should always be done slowly and intentionally. Even confident animals can become stressed when a new cat enters their space, so it is important to begin with separation. Keep your foster cat in a quiet room with their own food, water, litter box, and resting area for the first few days. Make sure any toys and bedding are washed and free of your current pet's scent. This allows the foster animal to adjust to their new environment and gain a sense of safety.  

For homes with resident cats, scent swapping is the safest first step. You can exchange blankets, toys, or gently rub each cat with a separate cloth to help them become familiar with each other’s smell. Once both cats seem calm and curious, you can allow brief visual introductions through a cracked door or baby gate. Hissing or growling is normal at first, but prolonged aggression or refusal to eat should be reported to the shelter before moving forward. Full face-to-face interactions should happen at least a day after introducing scents if not more, and only when both cats show relaxed body language and predictable behavior. After relaxed behavior is noticed, you can begin to introduce the cats through a barrier where they can see each other but remain in their separate boundaries. If signs of aggression or fear such as prolonged hissing, flared tails, swatting, or biting, separate the cats immediately, call the shelter, and try again another day. The next step would be to introduce the cats to each other in a supervised and neutral location, avoid using either cats space to prevent territorial aggression. Start with short interactions and use toys and treats to distract and reward for calm behavior. Continue to supervise interactions and be patient, it may take some cats weeks to months to adjust.  

For homes with resident dogs, it is especially important to ensure the dog is calm, leashed, and under full control during introductions. Many dogs are excited by small, fast‑moving animals, which can overwhelm a foster cat. Start by letting the dog sniff the foster cat’s scent under the door. When both animals appear comfortable, you can allow the dog to observe the cat from a distance while remaining seated and leashed. Reward calm behavior with treats and praise. Never allow a dog to chase, corner, or bark persistently at a foster cat, even in play. If the foster cat shows signs of fear such as hiding, flattening their ears, or refusing to come out, stop introductions and give them more time to adjust. 
Regardless of the species, the most important part of introducing pets is patience. Some animals adapt within days, while others may need weeks. Always follow the shelter’s guidance, especially if your foster cat has medical or behavioral notes that affect how they should be introduced. </p>

<h><b>Playing With Your Foster Cat<b></h>

<p> Play is an essential part of caring for your foster cat. It helps reduce their stress, builds confidence, and provide an outlet for natural hunting behaviors. You really don’t need expensive toys to keep a foster cat engaged. Simple, safe, and inexpensive options such as wand toys, crinkle balls, cardboard boxes, and paper bags without handles are often the most effective. Many cats also enjoy DIY toys like rolled‑up paper, toilet paper tubes, old receipts, or felt strips tied to a string, as long as they are supervised during play.

Wand toys are especially helpful for shy or energetic cats because they allow you to interact without overwhelming them. Move the toy in short, quick motions that mimic prey behavior, and let the cat “catch” it regularly to prevent frustration. For kittens, soft plush toys and lightweight balls encourage healthy exercise and coordination. Avoid toys with small detachable parts, loose strings, or anything that could be swallowed, as these can pose choking or intestinal hazards.

It is important to remember that hands and feet should never be used as toys. Allowing a foster cat to bite or scratch your skin during play can lead to behavioral issues as they grow, and it can make them less adoptable. Instead, redirect rough play to an appropriate toy. If a cat becomes overstimulated signaled by tail swishing, dilated pupils, or sudden biting pause the session and give them time to settle. 

Regular play sessions, even just 10–15 minutes twice a day, can make a significant difference in a foster cat’s confidence and overall well‑being. By offering safe toys and structured play, you help prepare them for a successful transition into their future home. </p>
